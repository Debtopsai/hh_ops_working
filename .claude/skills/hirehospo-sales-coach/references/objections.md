# Objection Playbook

The objections HireHospo's Meta stock-list leads actually raise, with compliance-safe answers.

**How to use this in a review:** find the objection in the transcript, compare the seller's
handling to the pattern below, and quote the replacement line. Do not read these out as scripts —
they are the *shape* of a good answer. A memorised line delivered at the wrong moment scores worse
than a clumsy one delivered at the right one.

## The universal shape

1. **Acknowledge** — repeat it back so they hear that you heard it. One sentence, no "but".
2. **Isolate** — *"If that were sorted, is there anything else holding you back?"* This is the step
   most calls skip, and it is the one that stops an hour of whack-a-mole.
3. **Answer** — on their terms, in their numbers, not with a brochure fact recited back.
4. **Confirm** — *"Does that deal with it?"* An unconfirmed objection comes back.

Skipping straight to (3) is the single most common objection-handling failure. It is technically
correct and feels, to the customer, like being argued with.

---

## "I just wanted the stock list" / "I'm just looking"

**The most common objection on these leads, and it is not really an objection.** It is an accurate
description of what they did. Treating it as resistance creates resistance.

Do not counter it. Agree, deliver the thing they asked for, and make the call worth their time:

> "Totally — and you should have it. The only reason I rang rather than just emailing it: the list
> changes weekly and half of it won't suit you. Two minutes and I can tell you which part to look
> at, and roughly what's realistic for a place like yours. If it's not the right time, that's
> completely fine."

**What it scores:** a seller who pushes past this ("so what equipment were you looking at?") is
scoring a 2 on Opening. A seller who agrees, honours the stock-list promise, and *still* earns two
minutes is a 5.

**Watch for:** the seller hearing this as rejection and rushing the close out of anxiety. It is the
opposite — the lead has just told you exactly where they are. And remember these leads named
specific equipment on the form; "just looking" is usually modesty, not disinterest. The form answer
is the way back in: *"You mentioned a chilled display — is that for a new site or replacing one?"*

## "How much is it?" — asked before credit approval

The trap objection. Answering it breaks gate **G1**; refusing badly loses the lead.

> "Straight answer: I can't give you a number yet, and I'd rather not guess and then have to walk
> it back. What it comes down to is the equipment, the term, and your deposit. The application's
> about three minutes, most come back inside 24 to 48 hours, and then I can show you the actual
> weekly — plus GST — rather than a maybe."

**Never:** "roughly around", "ballpark", "similar customers pay about". All of these are G1
breaches. A range is a quote.

**The reframe that works:** the credit check is not a hurdle standing between them and the number
— it is *the thing that produces* the number.

---

## "That's a long commitment" / "36 months is a lot"

Almost always a signal the wrong product was offered, or trading history was never established.

> "It can be — which is why there are two ways in. Lease-to-Own runs 36 months and you own the
> equipment at the end. Rent is 12 months, no ownership commitment, and at the end you can buy it
> at a discount, keep going at a lower rate, or upgrade. If you're early on, most people start
> there. How long have you been open?"

**Note the ending** — the answer closes by recovering the qualification fact that was missed. If
the seller had trading history, this objection largely doesn't arise.

---

## "Why would I finance when I could buy it outright?"

Do not argue against buying. Argue for the cash staying in the business.

> "If cash isn't tight, buying outright can absolutely be the right call. The reason most operators
> don't is that four thousand out of the account in week one is four thousand not covering stock,
> wages, or a quiet fortnight. The equipment earns from day one either way — the question is just
> whether you'd rather hold the cash. And with us it stays a weekly, plus GST, so it's a line on
> the roster, not a hole in the account."

**Scores badly:** any version that implies buying is foolish. These are operators; several have
been burned. Respect the option and reframe the cost.

---

## "Is it second-hand?" / "I don't want used equipment"

This is where margin is won or lost. **Refurbished is the offer.** A hesitant answer here confirms
the customer's fear.

> "Refurbished, and warranted — that's deliberate. It's what puts a Rational or an Electrolux in
> your kitchen at a weekly you'd otherwise pay for something entry-level. Washpro strips and
> rebuilds them here in New Zealand, and they come with warranty, so if anything goes wrong it's
> covered and they're the ones who service it. You're getting the premium brand without the
> premium capital."

**Automatic finding if:** the seller says "yes but", apologises, or offers a discount to compensate.
The correct posture is that refurbishment is *why* the deal is good.

For anything naming specific brands, models or price bands, defer to the **`hirehospo-products`**
skill — never invent equipment to win an objection.

---

## "The deposit is too much"

20 weeks upfront (10 rent in advance + 10 security) is real money for a startup. There are
alternative tiers — 6+6, 8+8, and 4+3 in special cases — but they are **credit-tier dependent and
not the seller's to promise.**

> "It is a chunk, and it's the part people push back on most. Standard is ten weeks in advance
> plus ten weeks security. There are lighter structures available depending on how the credit
> check comes back — I can't promise which one you'd land on before we run it, but it's worth
> knowing they exist. Shall we find out where you sit?"

**Breach if:** the seller names a specific alternative tier as available before credit is assessed.
That is a G3-adjacent promise about a credit outcome.

---

## "I need to talk to my partner / business partner"

Usually true, and usually a decision-maker fact that should have surfaced in qualification. Do not
fight it — make the absent person's decision easier.

> "Of course — it's their call as much as yours. What's the bit they'll want to understand? …
> I'll put that in writing so you're not relaying it second-hand. When are you two likely to sit
> down on it — is Thursday realistic for me to check back?"

**Scores a 5** when the seller extracts *what the partner will object to* and pre-answers it.
**Scores a 2** when the seller says "no problem, let me know" — that is also a 2 on Next Step.

---

## "Send me some information and I'll get back to you"

The polite close. Accepting it as-is is a **Next Step 2**.

> "I'll do that. So I send the right thing rather than everything — is it the equipment options
> you want to see, or the payment structures? … And so it doesn't sit in your inbox, I'll give you
> a ring Thursday morning. If you've decided against it by then, just tell me on the call and I'll
> close it off properly — no chasing."

Two moves: **narrow what's sent** (which forces a real answer about their interest) and **name the
callback day** while giving them explicit permission to say no.

---

## "I've had bad experiences with finance companies"

Do not defend the industry. Get specific.

> "That's fair, and I'd rather know now. What happened? … Right. The things I can tell you about
> us: it's a fixed weekly, plus GST — it doesn't move. The costs that exist are in the agreement
> before you sign, including the late fee and the admin fee, and I'll walk you through them rather
> than let you find them. And the servicing is Washpro here in New Zealand, not an offshore call
> centre."

**Transparency beats reassurance.** A seller who proactively names the 10% late fee and the
$25 + GST admin fee at this moment scores a 5. One who says "we're not like that" scores a 2.

---

## "Can you do better on the price?"

> "The weekly moves with the term and the deposit rather than with haggling — so yes, there's room,
> but it comes from structure. A longer term brings the weekly down; a larger deposit does too. Once
> credit's back I can show you two or three shapes and you can pick the one that fits your week."

Never discount the equipment to save a call. Move the structure.

---

## Silence / "not interested" / disengagement

If it comes early, the opener misfired — score Opening, not Objection Handling.

If it comes after a good discovery, one honest attempt:

> "No problem at all. Can I ask — is it the timing, or is it just not the right fit? It helps me
> know whether to close your file or check back in a few months."

Then stop. Chasing a genuine no burns the referral and the retarget.
