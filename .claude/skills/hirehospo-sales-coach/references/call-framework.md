# The HireHospo First Call

The shape a first call should take with a Meta stock-list lead, and where each stage breaks.
Use this to locate *where* a call went wrong before scoring *how badly*.

**Target length: 8–12 minutes.** Under 5 means qualification was skipped. Over 20 on a first call
usually means the seller pitched instead of qualifying, or is negotiating a deal that has no credit
approval behind it.

---

## Stage 0 · Before the phone rings

Not part of the call, but it decides the call.

**Speed is the whole game.** These are stock-list requests at roughly NZ$10 each, filled in on
Instagram or Facebook by someone who then moved on with their day. Every hour that passes costs contact rate. A
lead called within an hour is a different conversation from the same lead called on day three —
on day three the seller is explaining who HireHospo is; within the hour, the ad is still on screen.

Before dialling, know: **the ad they responded to**, the **form answer** they gave (often what
equipment they're after), their **company name**, and whether HubSpot shows them as already worked.

*Breaks here:* dialling cold with no idea which ad or campaign the lead came from, so the opener
can't reference anything the lead recognises.

---

## Stage 1 · Opener — 20 to 30 seconds

Three jobs: identify, **name the stock list**, get permission.

The ad promised "GET OUR LATEST STOCK LIST TODAY". Open exactly where that left them.

> "Hi Keryn, it's Urman from HireHospo — you asked for our stock list off Facebook over the
> weekend. Did that come through to you? … Good. The list is long and half of it won't suit you,
> so rather than let you wade through it I thought I'd find out what you're actually after and
> point you at the right part. Can I ask a couple of quick questions?"

If the list did **not** reach them, that is the opener:

> "…it looks like that never actually got to you, which is on us. I'll send it the moment we hang
> up. While I've got you — so I send the right section rather than all of it, what are you after?"

Naming the stock list does three things at once: it proves the call is not random, it matches the
lead's own memory of what they did, and it gives a reason for the call that serves *them*. Asking
permission costs four seconds and changes the register of everything after it.

*Breaks here:*
- Framing a stock-list request as a finance enquiry — the lead corrects you and starts on the back foot
- Calling it "your enquiry" — vague, and the lead cannot place it
- Never checking whether the promised list arrived, then being surprised by coolness
- Launching into equipment before permission
- Apologising for calling, which invites "actually now's not great"

## Stage 2 · Qualification — 2 to 4 minutes

Six facts (see `scorecard.md` D2). Get them conversationally. The order that flows best:

1. **"Tell me about the business — what have you got?"** → business type, and usually trading history free
2. **"How long have you been open?"** → the fact that decides the product. Never skip this
3. **"What's driving the equipment hunt?"** → need + urgency
4. **"When would you want it in?"** → timeframe
5. **"Is it just you calling the shots, or is there a partner in it?"** → decision maker
6. Deposit capacity — later, framed as structure rather than affordability

*Breaks here:*
- **Trading history never asked.** The most expensive single omission on these calls — without it
  the Rent vs Lease-to-Own recommendation is a guess, and getting it wrong reads as not listening
- Reading the form answer back as though it settles the need. *"Kitchen cookware, refrigerator and
  icebox"* is a tick-box, not a brief
- Interrogation rhythm — six questions with no reactions between them

---

## Stage 3 · Discovery — 2 to 4 minutes

Qualification gets *what*; discovery gets *why it matters*. Find the business consequence.

> "What are you doing at the moment without one? … And how long's that adding to close-down? …
> So that's what, an hour a night on wages?"

That sequence turns "I need a dishwasher" into "I'm paying someone an hour a night to do what a
machine does." The second is financeable; the first is shopping.

*Breaks here:*
- Stopping at the first answer. The second "why" is where the money is
- Hearing something important — a partner who decides, a lease ending, a machine that already
  failed, an opening date — and acknowledging it without exploring it. Listen for the quiet reveal
  at the three-minute mark
- Solving too early: the moment the seller starts recommending, discovery is over

---

## Stage 4 · Position the product — 1 to 2 minutes

Now, and only now, put a shape on it. Payment-led, product matched to trading history.

> "Based on what you've said — open eight months, and you want the option to own it eventually —
> there are two ways this goes. Rent is 12 months, lower commitment, and at the end you can buy it
> at a discount, carry on cheaper, or upgrade. Lease-to-Own is 36 months, fixed weekly, and it's
> yours at the end. Both are a weekly payment plus GST rather than four grand out of the account.
> Off what you've told me I'd lean toward [X] — but let's get the credit check done before I put
> numbers on it."

*Breaks here:*
- **Any weekly figure.** Gate G1. This is the highest-frequency compliance breach on these calls,
  and it happens because the customer asks and the seller wants to be helpful
- Leading with equipment price ("it's a $4,000 machine") — sells the price, not the payment
- Offering 36-month Lease-to-Own to a business trading six weeks
- Apologising for refurbishment when it comes up (see `objections.md`)

---

## Stage 5 · The credit ask — 1 minute

The actual close of a first call. Frame it as fast, free, and the thing that unlocks the number.

> "Next step's a quick application — about three minutes, and most come back within 24 to 48 hours.
> Once it's through I can show you the real weekly rather than a guess. Want me to send it now
> while we're on the phone?"

*Breaks here:*
- Never asked, so the call has no path to a quote and the "next step" is a follow-up email
- Framed as a hurdle or a risk ("we'd have to run a credit check on you") — invites hesitation
- Asked so late it lands after the customer has mentally left

---

## Stage 6 · Next step — 30 seconds

Specific, dated, mutual, repeated back.

> "So: I'll send the application through in the next five minutes. You'll get it done tonight.
> I'll call you Thursday morning around ten with the outcome and the numbers. That work?"

*Breaks here:*
- "I'll send you some info and follow up" — no date, closes nothing
- The date is one-sided: the seller commits, the customer doesn't
- Ending on "have a think about it"

---

## Stage 7 · Close-off

Confirm the email address out loud. Thank them for the time. Do not re-pitch after the close —
calls that reopen after the next step is agreed tend to unpick it.

---

## Reading the whole call

Four questions after the transcript, before scoring:

1. **Did the seller earn the right to ask questions?** (Stage 1)
2. **Could someone else run this deal from what was established?** If the notes wouldn't let a
   colleague pick it up, qualification failed regardless of how warm it felt
3. **Was a number said before credit?** (Gate G1)
4. **Is there a dated commitment from *both* sides?** (Stage 6)

Three or four yeses is a good call. The most common real-world profile is a warm, likeable call
that fails 2 and 4 — pleasant, and worth nothing on Thursday.
